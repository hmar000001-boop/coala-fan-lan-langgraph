\chapter{Persistent Intention and Resource Governance: B4 and B6}
\label{ch:persistent-intention-resource-governance}

\section*{Chapter at a glance}
\phantomsection
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Chapter at a glance}

A supply-chain recovery objective can remain authorized even when its current method is no longer suitable. The planner may need a different search strategy, while uncertainty or a shrinking computational budget may require the next attempt to use fewer resources, request evidence, wait for a human decision, or stop. These are related decisions, but they are not the same decision. B4 governs the persistence of the intention and the authority to reconsider its method. B6 governs uncertainty and resource consequences. Neither mechanism grants the capability to execute a reservation, supplier commitment, or other state-changing action; the application still passes through its separate authorization, approval, and revalidation boundary.

This chapter follows those distinctions through the current Java application. It begins with the durable B4 intention record, then follows method reconsideration through the lifecycle service, PostgreSQL compare-and-set, the cognitive-cycle graph, and the next planner call. It then traces B6's scoped uncertainty assessment, optional calibration lifecycle, resource vector, planner downshift, and fenced execution guard. The chapter treats the code as an application-specific engineering realization of the mechanisms discussed in Part II, not as a complete BDI, MIDCA, EPIC, or Sigma implementation.

\begin{learningbox}
By the end of this chapter, you should be able to distinguish a committed objective from its active method; explain how B4 changes a method without changing the objective fingerprint; trace the version and persistence checks that protect an intention transition; explain how B6 binds uncertainty to an execution and memory scope; distinguish a calibrated assessment from an uncalibrated or research-only shadow value; explain the resource vector and the difference between planner downshift and the shared execution ceiling; trace a reservation lease from acquisition through operation start and accounting; and identify why B4, B6, and action authorization remain separate control boundaries.
\end{learningbox}

\section{The task can remain valid when its current method no longer is}
\label{sec:ch23-valid-task-method-change}

\BookExampleLabel{} Suppose the application is coordinating recovery for several delayed customer orders. The authorized objective remains to fulfill those orders while preserving the constraints already attached to the request. A sequential planner has produced a candidate, but fresh evidence makes its current route or allocation method unattractive. The objective need not be discarded merely because one method has become unsuitable. The system may reconsider the method and select a bounded-search strategy while keeping the same protected objective.

A second problem may arise at the same time. The next planning step is computationally expensive, the remaining search budget is low, or the available evidence carries high uncertainty. The application must decide whether to continue, reduce the next planning call's compute envelope, request more evidence, interrupt for an authorized human decision, or stop. It must not silently convert that resource decision into permission to change the business objective.

The questions are therefore separate:

\begin{description}
  \item[B4 — intention and method.] Does the committed objective remain active? Is the current method still acceptable? Which authorized transition changes the method, suspends the intention, returns from a delegated investigation, or ends the intention after the required evidence and authority checks?
  \item[B6 — uncertainty and resources.] Is the current uncertainty assessment applicable to this execution and scope? Does the next step fit the remaining resource vector and deadline? Should the system continue, request evidence, downshift, wait for a human decision, or stop?
  \item[Action authorization — capability and approval.] Even when B4 permits the method and B6 permits work to continue, is this exact proposed state-changing action authorized, approved where required, and still valid when rechecked?
\end{description}

The separation matters because the same planner method may be used under a smaller budget, and a newly selected method may still produce a proposal that must not be executed. B4 does not grant a supplier-write capability. B6 does not authenticate an operator. Neither one replaces the M3 authorization, idempotency, approval, and observation path. The application graph makes these decisions sequentially visible rather than blending them into one confidence or policy score.\FanLanCite{\S V-C, B4; \S V-E, B6; \S VI-A; Appendix C-C}

\section{B4 state: a protected objective and a versioned method binding}
\label{sec:ch23-b4-state}

\subsection{The intention is more than a planner choice}
\label{subsec:ch23-intention-record}

The central record is \texttt{RecoveryIntention}. It identifies the intention, memory scope, recovery objective, objective fingerprint, objective owner, protected constraints, current method, lifecycle status, and monotonically increasing version. It also records rationale, the active plan reference, continuation conditions, permitted reconsideration triggers, an authority policy, and terminal metadata where applicable. The record is therefore not merely the selected planner ID. It is the persisted commitment that makes the method binding accountable to an objective and an authority context.

The objective fingerprint is a stable digest of the committed recovery objective. A method change supplies an asserted fingerprint, and the transition rejects a mismatch. The method reference may change; the fingerprint may not. This distinction is the key invariant: the planner is allowed to change how it pursues the authorized task, not to redefine the task as a side effect of switching strategy.

The status vocabulary is explicit: \texttt{ACTIVE}, \texttt{RECONSIDERATION\_PENDING}, \texttt{SUSPENDED}, \texttt{DELEGATED}, \texttt{COMPLETED}, and \texttt{TERMINATED}. Suspension preserves the status to which the intention should resume. Delegation carries a child-case identifier only while the intention is delegated. Completion carries an objective-satisfaction proof whose fingerprint must match the protected objective. Termination carries a typed reason and actor. These structural constraints make several invalid combinations unrepresentable at record construction time.

\subsection{Reconsideration precedes a method change}
\label{subsec:ch23-reconsideration-method}

The current method cannot be replaced directly from an arbitrary lifecycle state. The record first requires an active intention and a permitted typed trigger to enter \texttt{RECONSIDERATION\_PENDING}. A later method change is accepted only from that state, only when the asserted objective fingerprint matches, and only when the actor belongs to the intention's transition policy. The new \texttt{RecoveryMethodReference} becomes the current method and supplies the next candidate-plan reference; the intention returns to \texttt{ACTIVE} with its objective identity preserved.

\texttt{RecoveryIntentionService} is the version-aware application service. Its public method delegates to a common update path. That path loads the current value, checks the caller's expected version, applies the typed transition, classifies it, and asks the repository to compare-and-set the replacement. Only after the repository reports a successful commit does the service append the transition to its recorder. A stale expected version or losing concurrent writer is rejected; it is not merged by overwriting the latest intention.

\Needspace{0.90\textheight}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Java,basicstyle=\ttfamily\footnotesize,breaklines=true,caption={Method replacement is a typed transition; the shared update path uses the expected intention version and a repository compare-and-set.},label={lst:ch23-b4-method-cas}]
public RecoveryIntention changeMethod(RecoveryIntentionId id, long version,
        RecoveryMethodReference method, String objectiveFingerprint,
        String actor, Instant at) {
    return update(id, version, actor,
            value -> value.changeMethod(method, objectiveFingerprint, actor, at));
}

private RecoveryIntention update(RecoveryIntentionId id, long expected,
        String actor, UnaryOperator<RecoveryIntention> operation) {
    String transitionActor = required(actor, "actor");
    RecoveryIntention current = get(Objects.requireNonNull(id, "id"));
    if (current.version() != expected)
        throw new IllegalStateException("intention version conflict");
    RecoveryIntention next = operation.apply(current);
    var kind = transitionKind(current, next);
    if (!repository.compareAndSet(id, expected, next))
        throw new IllegalStateException("intention changed concurrently");
    transitionRecorder.record(current, next, kind, transitionActor);
    return next;
}

The PostgreSQL repository adds a second, durable concurrency check. Its update predicate includes persistence scope, intention ID, expected version, and objective fingerprint. When one row changes, the new version is appended to the intention-history table inside the same JDBC transaction. If no row matches, the transaction rolls back and the caller receives a failed compare-and-set. This makes the current record and its transition-history entry one database transaction; it does not create a distributed transaction with the B2 case repository, the graph checkpoint, or an external action system.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Java,basicstyle=\ttfamily\footnotesize,breaklines=true,caption={The PostgreSQL B4 repository guards the versioned replacement by scope, ID, expected version, and objective fingerprint, then writes history before commit.},label={lst:ch23-b4-postgres-cas}]
@Override public boolean compareAndSet(RecoveryIntentionId id, long expectedVersion, RecoveryIntention replacement) {
    Objects.requireNonNull(id, "id"); Objects.requireNonNull(replacement, "replacement");
    if (!id.equals(replacement.id())) throw new SecurityException("replacement intention ID changed");
    if (scopeId != null && !scopeId.equals(replacement.scopeId().value())) throw new SecurityException("replacement intention belongs to another persistence scope");
    if (replacement.version() != expectedVersion + 1) throw new IllegalArgumentException("replacement intention version must increment by one");
    String payload = PostgresJsonArtifactRepository.validJson(codec.encode(replacement));
    try (Connection connection = dataSource.getConnection()) {
        connection.setAutoCommit(false);
        try {
            int changed;
            try (PreparedStatement update = connection.prepareStatement("""
                    UPDATE recovery_intention SET version=?,status=?,payload=CAST(? AS jsonb),updated_at=?
                    WHERE scope_id=? AND intention_id=? AND version=? AND objective_fingerprint=?
                    """)) {
                update.setLong(1, replacement.version()); update.setString(2, replacement.status().name());
                update.setString(3, payload); PostgresJdbc.setInstant(update, 4, replacement.updatedAt());
                update.setString(5, replacement.scopeId().value()); update.setString(6, id.value());
                update.setLong(7, expectedVersion); update.setString(8, replacement.objectiveFingerprint());
                changed = update.executeUpdate();
            }
            if (changed == 0) { connection.rollback(); return false; }
            appendHistory(connection, replacement, payload);
            connection.commit(); return true;
        } catch (SQLException | RuntimeException failure) { rollback(connection, failure); throw failure; }
    } catch (SQLException ex) { throw new PostgresPersistenceException("PostgreSQL B4 intention CAS failed", ex); }
}

\section{B4 runtime governance: the new method must reach the next planner call}
\label{sec:ch23-b4-runtime-governance}

\subsection{The domain supplies a directive; the lifecycle service enforces it}
\label{subsec:ch23-b4-govern-intention}

The lifecycle service governs the intention before the next planner call. It first requires a current uncertainty assessment, loads the persisted intention and the selected plan reference, and checks the version carried in graph state. One narrow replay exception is allowed: if the graph checkpoint is exactly one intention version behind because the active-plan binding committed before the checkpoint update, the runtime accepts only that matching active-plan replay. An unrelated concurrent B4 transition causes a reload error rather than silent reconciliation.

If the intention is active and the selected plan reference differs, the lifecycle service binds the selected plan through a new versioned transition. It then asks the domain port for an \texttt{IntentionDirective}. This is an important boundary: the lifecycle service does not invent a domain method preference from a model reflection. The domain supplies a typed action, actor, reason, trigger, replacement method and asserted objective fingerprint as applicable. The service validates the current state and routes the directive through the B4 service. Termination receives an additional host authorization check; only after that check does the runtime call the special host-authorized termination path.

\subsection{A method change is observable at the planner boundary}
\label{subsec:ch23-next-planner-call}

After B4 governance, the outer graph updates the intention ID and version, method ID, candidate-plan reference, and objective fingerprint in graph state. It discards a planner session when the planner authority has changed or when the directive requested reconsideration or method change. The intention node routes \texttt{CONTINUE} to B6 meta-control, routes \texttt{RECONSIDER} and \texttt{CHANGE\_METHOD} back to context loading and planning, sends suspension to human control, and sends termination to the completion path. The graph therefore gives a method change a concrete consequence: the next planner call receives a new B4 binding rather than merely seeing a log entry.

The full-graph test starts with \texttt{COT\_SEQUENTIAL}, requests reconsideration, and then changes the method to \texttt{tot}. The next planning invocation uses \texttt{TOT\_BOUNDED\_SEARCH}; the planner override carries the same objective fingerprint, the registered method ID and candidate-plan reference, and the current intention identity. The previous planner session is not resumed under the changed method. This is the test's demonstrated behavior, not evidence that the new planner is more effective on a broad workload.

\subsection{B2 delegation and termination remain separate transitions}
\label{subsec:ch23-b4-b2-handoff}

An active B4 intention may delegate a bounded investigation to B2. While delegated, the intention records the child case ID and enters \texttt{DELEGATED}; Chapter 22 covers the case, its child-step controls, and parent-return validation. When the result returns, \texttt{returnFromDelegation(...)} checks that the intention is still delegated to that exact child and that the actor is permitted. A \texttt{NO\_METHOD\_CHANGE} return restores \texttt{ACTIVE}; a return requiring a new method decision enters \texttt{RECONSIDERATION\_PENDING}, and only when the configured trigger set includes \texttt{DELEGATED\_INVESTIGATION\_RETURNED}. The child result does not itself replace the parent objective.

Opening a B2 case, delegating B4, and starting the child are operations across separate contracts and repositories. If a later step fails, the runtime uses compensating transitions where available. That is not a distributed atomic transaction: a process failure can occur between the separately committed operations, and reconciliation must inspect their durable states rather than assume a single cross-store commit.

Termination has a stronger boundary than ordinary method replacement. The default authority policy requires independent host authorization for termination. In the graph path, the runtime calls its host authority port to authenticate and authorize the actor before it uses \texttt{terminateAfterHostAuthorization}. An intention cannot be terminated by an arbitrary model-generated sentence, nor can objective satisfaction be claimed through a termination action. Completion requires an \texttt{ObjectiveSatisfactionProof} that matches the protected fingerprint.

\section{B6 state: uncertainty is scoped, versioned, and evidence-bound}
\label{sec:ch23-b6-uncertainty}

\subsection{The graph carries a reference, not the authoritative assessment}
\label{subsec:ch23-uncertainty-reference}

\texttt{UncertaintyAssessment} is an immutable assessment containing its reference, assessment time, validity deadline, provenance reference, calibration metadata, and one signal for every declared uncertainty dimension. Its constructor rejects a missing dimension, a key/value dimension mismatch, and unsupported dimensions. The assessment reference contains an assessment ID, positive version, execution ID, and memory-scope ID. The graph carries this compact reference between nodes; the host-owned assessment store remains the authoritative home of the assessment and its provenance.

The \texttt{isCurrentFor(...)} check requires the reference's execution and memory scope to match the current context and requires the current time to fall inside the assessment's validity interval. A number in the interval [0,1] is not automatically a calibrated probability. The record says so explicitly: calibration status must carry a profile ID, target, positive profile version, validation-evidence reference, and positive validation sample count. A \texttt{NOT\_CALIBRATED} assessment cannot carry those claims.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Java,basicstyle=\ttfamily\footnotesize,breaklines=true,caption={The uncertainty reference is checkpoint-safe identity. The full assessment remains scoped to its execution and validity window.},label={lst:ch23-uncertainty-reference}]
public boolean isCurrentFor(String executionId, String memoryScopeId, Instant now) {
    Objects.requireNonNull(now, "now");
    return reference.executionId().equals(executionId)
            && reference.memoryScopeId().equals(memoryScopeId)
            && !now.isBefore(assessedAt)
            && now.isBefore(validUntil);
}

\subsection{Assessment triggers and calibration lifecycle}
\label{subsec:ch23-uncertainty-assessment-lifecycle}

The runtime asks the domain for a new assessment using a stable trigger ID together with the execution ID, memory scope, and previous assessment reference to derive an idempotency key. The returned assessment must belong to the current execution and scope and be valid at the current time. The first revision must be version 1. A later revision must retain the same assessment ID and advance exactly one version. When B6 later governs the cycle, its reference must equal the exact reference carried by graph state; a stale or foreign assessment is rejected rather than silently substituted.

Calibration is conditional. \texttt{CognitiveCycle\allowbreak{}LifecycleServices} accepts an optional \texttt{Uncertainty\allowbreak{}CalibrationService}; the constructor can store \texttt{null}. If the domain claims that an assessment is already calibrated, the lifecycle service checks that the named profile and version are active for the exact target and memory scope. If the assessment is not calibrated and the optional service is configured, the service attempts calibration against an active scope/target-matched profile. If no active profile is found, the raw assessment is returned unchanged. A research-only shadow profile may transform a disposable view, but it remains marked \texttt{NOT\_CALIBRATED} and is not persisted as an operational calibrated assessment.

The calibration service separates training, independent validation, and activation. It persists a new trained profile; validation consumes a holdout set and an identified reviewer; activation uses a compare-and-set that replaces the active profile for the target. The profile's calibration method checks active status and scope, while the lifecycle validates target and version binding before accepting a calibrated assessment. A persisted assessment is therefore not proof that the underlying model is well calibrated. Only an active, validated, target- and scope-matched profile can support the status label. The relevant tests establish lifecycle checks and selected calibration behavior; they do not establish calibration quality on live operational data.

\section{B6 resource governance: a vector, not a single allowance}
\label{sec:ch23-b6-resource-governance}

\subsection{The budget units are computational, not business inventory}
\label{subsec:ch23-resource-vector}

The resource vector contains discrete computational and execution units such as \texttt{MODEL\_CALLS}, \texttt{INPUT\_TOKENS}, \texttt{OUTPUT\_TOKENS}, \texttt{REASONING\_EVALUATIONS}, \texttt{SEARCH\_EXPANSIONS}, \texttt{MCTS\_ROLLOUTS}, \texttt{EXTERNAL\_READS}, \texttt{VERIFICATION\_CALLS}, and \texttt{ACTION\_\allowbreak DISPATCHES}. These units are not interchangeable. A planning request may consume search expansions and model calls; a reasoning invocation may consume a model call and a reasoning evaluation; an action-dispatch adapter may charge a dispatch after the separate M3 idempotency ledger grants the attempt.

Inventory, money, supplier capacity, shipment capacity, and order quantities are business-domain resources, not B6 computational budget units. A low search-expansion balance cannot be used to reinterpret an inventory shortage, and a planner downshift does not enlarge a supplier's physical capacity. Those domain facts remain governed by the supply-chain model and simulator. The B6 ledger is an additional execution constraint, not the business-world resource model.

\subsection{The governor returns a typed control decision}
\label{subsec:ch23-meta-control-governor}
\Needspace{0.88\textheight}

\texttt{MetaControlGovernor.evaluate(...)} consumes a budget snapshot, a current uncertainty assessment, the resource usage required by the next step, and the current time. Its precedence is explicit. It stops if actual consumption is already over budget or the deadline has passed. It interrupts for a human decision if the next step cannot fit the remaining budget, or if the maximum uncertainty reaches the human-review threshold. It requests evidence when uncertainty reaches the evidence threshold. If uncertainty is below those thresholds but the remaining margin for a required finite resource is low, it returns \texttt{REDUCE\_COMPUTATION} with a typed \texttt{PlannerBudgetDownshift}. Otherwise, it returns \texttt{CONTINUE}.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Java,basicstyle=\ttfamily\footnotesize,breaklines=true,caption={B6 applies an ordered policy: exhaustion and deadline first, then feasibility and uncertainty, then a bounded planner downshift.},label={lst:ch23-meta-control-governor}]
public MetaControlDecision evaluate(ResourceBudgetSnapshot budget, UncertaintyAssessment uncertainty,
                                    ResourceUsage requiredForNextStep, Instant now) {
    Objects.requireNonNull(budget, "budget");
    Objects.requireNonNull(uncertainty, "uncertainty");
    Objects.requireNonNull(requiredForNextStep, "requiredForNextStep");
    Objects.requireNonNull(now, "now");
    double max = uncertainty.maximumUncertainty();
    if (budget.overBudget())
    return decision(MetaControlAction.STOP, MetaControlReason.REQUIRED_RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED,
            ResumeCondition.NEVER, "actual resource consumption exceeded its configured limit", max);
if (!now.isBefore(budget.budget().deadline()))
    return decision(MetaControlAction.STOP, MetaControlReason.DEADLINE_EXCEEDED,
            ResumeCondition.NEVER, "execution deadline has passed", max);

        .anyMatch(unit -> requiredForNextStep.amount(unit) > budget.remaining(unit));
if (nextStepBlocked)
    return decision(MetaControlAction.INTERRUPT_FOR_HUMAN,
            MetaControlReason.REQUIRED_RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED, ResumeCondition.BUDGET_REPLENISHED,
            "the next step cannot fit the remaining resource budget; wait for explicitly authorized replenishment", max);
if (max >= policy.humanReviewUncertainty())
    return decision(MetaControlAction.INTERRUPT_FOR_HUMAN,

if (max >= policy.evidenceRequestUncertainty())
    return decision(MetaControlAction.REQUEST_EVIDENCE,
            MetaControlReason.UNCERTAINTY_REQUIRES_EVIDENCE, ResumeCondition.NEW_EVIDENCE,
            "additional evidence is required before continuing", max);

        .filter(unit -> requiredForNextStep.amount(unit) > 0
                && budget.budget().limit(unit) != Long.MAX_VALUE && budget.budget().limit(unit) > 0)
        .anyMatch(unit -> (double) budget.remaining(unit) / budget.budget().limit(unit)

            max, new PlannerBudgetDownshift(policy.plannerBudgetReductionPercent(),
                    "b6-resource-margin-downshift-v1"));

\section{Execution enforcement: reservation, lease, fencing, and accounting}
\label{sec:ch23-execution-enforcement}

\subsection{The shared guard reserves before invoking work}
\label{subsec:ch23-budget-execution-guard}

A planner downshift reduces one planner request, but B6 also needs a separate runtime enforcement boundary for measured operations. The shared execution guard is used by planning and reasoning paths. Its constructor requires a \texttt{LeasedResourceBudgetLedger}; a ledger without lease and fencing capability is rejected. Each operation receives a deterministic reservation identity derived from the execution and operation IDs, and the guard uses a process-local owner ID plus a monotonically increasing fencing token. The durable operation-start transition is committed before the guarded operation runs.

The central ordering is reserve, acquire an active lease, mark the operation started, then invoke the operation while renewing the lease. If the operation throws after it has started, the guard conservatively commits the reserved amount rather than releasing work that may already have consumed resources. If the trusted measurement fails or returns null, it also charges the reservation conservatively. When a valid measurement is available, the actual usage is committed. The heartbeat is cancelled in every exit path. This policy favors accounting safely over pretending that a failed measurement means no resources were consumed.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Java,basicstyle=\ttfamily\footnotesize,breaklines=true,caption={The shared guard obtains a fenced reservation and commits operation start before the provider is invoked.},label={lst:ch23-resource-budget-execution-guard}]

                     Supplier<T> operation, Function<T, ResourceUsage> actualUsage) {

    Objects.requireNonNull(reserved, "reserved");
    Objects.requireNonNull(operation, "operation");
    Objects.requireNonNull(actualUsage, "actualUsage");

            "host returned no B6 budget for execution");

    String reservationId = reservationIdFor(execution, operationKey);
    BudgetReservationLease lease = leasedLedger.acquireLease(execution, reservationId, ownerId,
            reserved, acquiredAt.plus(leaseDuration), acquiredAt);

        throw new BudgetReservationInUseException("budgeted operation was already completed or conservatively reconciled; refusing to invoke it again");
    if (lease.status() != ReservationStatus.ACTIVE || !ownerId.equals(lease.ownerId()) || lease.operationStarted())
        throw new BudgetReservationInUseException("budgeted operation could not obtain an unstarted reservation lease");

            lease.fencingToken(), clock.instant());

\subsection{PostgreSQL leases make ownership and expiry explicit}
\label{subsec:ch23-postgres-resource-lease}

The PostgreSQL ledger serializes budget-account operations by locking the execution's budget row inside a transaction. It checks the deadline, performs expired-reservation recovery, and loads an existing reservation. The same reservation ID cannot be reused for a different resource amount. A committed operation is returned as committed and the runtime guard refuses to invoke it again. A live active lease blocks a competing owner. Only a released reservation whose recovery outcome is \texttt{EXPIRED\_BEFORE\_START} and which never crossed the operation-start boundary is eligible for reclamation. Reclaiming it increments the fencing token. A new reservation starts with fencing token 1.

The subsequent \texttt{markOperationStarted}, \texttt{renewLease}, and \texttt{commitOwned} methods require the exact owner and fencing token, and the lease must remain valid. An expired started reservation is not treated like an unstarted reservation that can be safely reclaimed. The recovery policy conservatively accounts for started work because the runtime cannot infer from lease expiry alone that a provider invocation did not happen.

\section{The graph keeps B4, B6, and action authorization in separate lanes}
\label{sec:ch23-separated-control-lanes}

The outer cognitive-cycle graph exposes separate nodes named \texttt{b4-intention-\allowbreak governance} and \texttt{b6-meta-\allowbreak control}. After the planner evaluates a candidate, the graph refreshes uncertainty before entering B4 intention governance. A B4 \texttt{CONTINUE} proceeds to B6. A B4 reconsideration or method change returns to context loading and planning; suspension waits for human control; termination follows the completion path; other outcomes escalate. After B6, only \texttt{CONTINUE} routes to the authorization node. Evidence requests wait for evidence, a downshift returns to planning, interruption waits for a human decision, and the remaining terminal outcome escalates.

A human response is authenticated against a host-issued response reference and actor ID. After human control supplies evidence or authorizes a downshift/continuation, the graph refreshes uncertainty before running B6 again. When B6 returns \texttt{CONTINUE}, the next node is action authorization. The action authorization path can return \texttt{NO\_APPROVAL\_REQUIRED} or require explicit approval; the graph then revalidates the plan before execution. A method change or a resource downshift therefore does not skip the action boundary.

\section{B5 and GraSP remain a separate boundary}
\label{sec:ch23-b5-boundary}

This chapter does not introduce a GraSP implementation. Part II Chapter 17 closed the bounded B5/GraSP review as a separate candidate boundary. The application's procedural memory, B2 recovery-procedure draft and promotion path, B1 retrieval, and B4/B6 lifecycle services should not be relabelled as GraSP. \section{B4 and B6 compose on the same subsequent planner call}
\label{sec:ch23-combined-b4-b6}

The clearest combined evidence is a full-graph integration test for the B4/B6 composition. It starts from the sequential planner, requests reconsideration, changes the method to the registered \texttt{tot} method, and then observes a subsequent planner invocation under B6 downshift. The controlled invocation still uses \texttt{TOT\_BOUNDED\_SEARCH}; its expansion and evaluation budgets are halved, and the planner-selection override still carries the unchanged objective fingerprint. B6 reduces compute without overriding the method selected by B4.

A second full-graph test isolates the resource effect. It verifies that the first planning call receives the baseline envelope and the second receives half the expansion, evaluation, model-call, and rollout limits. It also checks that maximum search depth, maximum candidate count, and the absolute deadline remain unchanged. The test then reads the shared execution ledger: its search-expansion ceiling remains 100, consumed work is at least 80, and no reservation remains in flight. The distinction is observable in test assertions, not merely explained in prose.

The PostgreSQL integration test adds a different kind of evidence: planning and M3 reasoning charge one shared durable account, and the closed-store test proves the reasoning provider is not invoked before the unavailable budget store can enforce the reservation. These tests demonstrate selected full-graph and persistence paths. They do not establish production service-level objectives, the quality of calibration on a live distribution, or empirical improvement in supply-chain outcomes.

The evidence is deliberately layered. Unit tests cover local record and governor invariants; runtime tests cover guard behavior; full-graph tests cover the effect on the next planner call; PostgreSQL integration tests cover selected persistence and fail-closed paths. Passing tests establish those assertions for the tested configuration. They do not certify deployment, calibration quality, concurrency performance, or empirical supply-chain benefit.

\begin{chapterrecap}
B4 preserves a committed recovery objective while governing its method and intention lifecycle. The objective fingerprint and protected constraints remain fixed across typed reconsideration and method change. The service checks actor and version, and the PostgreSQL repository applies a compare-and-set guarded by scope, ID, expected version, and objective fingerprint while appending history in the same transaction. The runtime receives a domain-supplied directive and makes the resulting method visible to the next planner call; a full-graph test shows the change from sequential reasoning to bounded tree search without changing the objective. Delegation to B2, return from the child, completion, and host-authorized termination remain separate lifecycle transitions.

B6 consumes a scoped and versioned uncertainty assessment together with a resource snapshot and the next step's estimated requirements. The assessment is not automatically a calibrated probability, and the calibration service is optional in host composition. The governor chooses among continue, request evidence, reduce computation, interrupt for human control, or stop according to explicit precedence. A planner downshift reduces the next planning call but leaves the shared execution ceiling unchanged. Actual planning, reasoning, and action-dispatch work can be subjected to a shared reservation and fencing guard; started work is conservatively charged when its outcome or measurement is not trustworthy enough for release. The PostgreSQL test demonstrates shared accounting for M5 planning and M3 reasoning in the tested wiring, while the action path retains the separate M3 idempotency, authorization, approval, and reconciliation boundaries.

B4, B6, and action authorization are complementary but non-interchangeable. B4 cannot grant an action capability; B6 cannot change the goal or authenticate an operator; and remaining budget cannot replace a capability grant or an approval. These Java contracts are an application-specific engineering interpretation of Fan and Lan's mechanism framing. They are not complete reproductions of BDI, MIDCA, EPIC, or Sigma, and they do not implement the closed B5/GraSP candidate.
\end{chapterrecap}
